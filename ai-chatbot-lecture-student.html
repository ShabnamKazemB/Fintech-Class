Large Language Models

The mechanics of AI chatbots: tokenisation, inference, architecture and training

This lecture examines how an AI chatbot generates a response, from the tokenisation of the user's input to the model that produces the output.

The underlying model does not search the internet or consult a database. It generates text by repeatedly predicting the next token. Some commercial chatbot products add a separate retrieval step on top of the model; this is a feature of the product rather than of the model itself.

Layer 1: Tokenisation

The model operates on numbers, not words

Input text is segmented into units called tokens, each of which is assigned an integer identifier:

Identifiers are illustrative. Production tokenisers typically have vocabularies of approximately 50,000 to 200,000 tokens.

Note that a less frequent word such as “compressed” may be split into sub-word units. Each identifier is then mapped to a high-dimensional vector (an embedding), on which all subsequent computation is performed.

Text is segmented into tokens, which may be whole words, sub-word units or individual characters. Each token receives an integer identifier and is then represented as a vector of real numbers (an embedding). Less frequent words are commonly split into several tokens. All subsequent operations are numerical computations on these vectors.

Layer 2: Prediction

Text generation as sequential forecasting

The selected token is appended to the sequence and the forecast is repeated. A complete response is the outcome of several hundred iterations of this procedure.

Candidate next tokenProbability
At each step the model computes a probability for every token in its vocabulary, conditional on the preceding sequence: P(next token | previous tokens). A token is selected, appended to the sequence, and the process repeats. This is termed inference, or autoregressive generation. The model does not plan the complete response in advance.

Layer 2: Prediction

Why identical prompts yield different responses

The temperature parameter governs randomness in token selection: low values favour the most probable token, whereas high values admit less probable ones.

Low temperature

Near-deterministic and reproducible; suited to formal reporting.

High temperature

More diverse, but more prone to error.

Temperature rescales the probability distribution before sampling. At low temperature the most probable token is selected almost invariably, producing consistent output. At high temperature less probable tokens are selected more often, producing more varied output with a higher incidence of error.

Layer 3: Architecture

The fundamental building block: linear regression

From econometrics:

y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂

A single artificial neuron:

output = f( w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b )

A weighted sum of inputs is passed through a non-linear activation function f, which allows the network to approximate non-linear relationships.

An artificial neuron is analogous to a linear regression: each input is multiplied by a weight, a bias term is added, and the result is passed through a non-linear activation function. Stacking many layers of such neurons yields a neural network capable of approximating highly complex, non-linear relationships.

Layer 3: The transformer

Attention: how the model weights context

Select the word bank in each sentence.

Shading indicates the attention weight assigned by “bank” to each word (illustrative values).

  • Attention: each token computes a relevance score for every other token in the sequence.
  • Depth: the operation is repeated across many layers, progressively refining each token's contextual representation.
  • Position: positional encoding preserves word order, so that “bank lends to firm” is distinguished from “firm lends to bank”.
By analogy, an analyst reading an earnings report infers from the surrounding terms whether “margin” refers to profit margin or to margin lending.
Contemporary LLMs are based on the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017). Its central mechanism is attention: each token assesses the relevance of every other token, so that the same word, such as "bank", acquires a different representation in a different context. The model also encodes word order (positional encoding) and refines each token's representation across many layers.

Layer 3: Architecture

Linear regression and large language models compared

Linear regressionLarge language model
ParametersA small number of coefficientsUp to hundreds of billions of weights
InputsExplanatory variables, e.g. interest rates, GDP, inflationThe token sequence generated to date
Interaction of inputsFixed, additive effects unless interaction terms are specifiedContext-dependent weighting of every token (attention)
OutputA point estimate, e.g. expected returnA probability distribution over the vocabulary
EstimationMinimise the sum of squared residualsMinimise cross-entropy loss (equivalently, maximise likelihood)
InterpretabilityHigh: each coefficient has a direct interpretationLow: individual weights lack a direct interpretation (the “black box” problem)
An LLM may be viewed as a non-linear extension of regression at an enormous scale. Unlike a regression coefficient, however, its individual parameters cannot be meaningfully interpreted in isolation; hence the characterisation of such models as black boxes.

The update rule is gradient descent: a numerical optimisation method that moves iteratively in the direction of steepest decrease of the loss function. It is conceptually analogous to least-squares estimation, although no closed-form solution exists.

Trillionsof tokens of training text (published estimates for frontier models)
Thousandsof specialised processors operating for weeks or months
Human feedbackapplied after pre-training to improve helpfulness and safety
Training repeats a single procedure: predict the next token, measure the error with a loss function (cross-entropy), and update the weights by gradient descent. This is analogous in spirit to least-squares estimation. Following this pre-training phase, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is used to improve the helpfulness and safety of the model.

Layer 5: Limitations

Fluency does not imply accuracy

User
What was Harbourview Savings Bank’s net profit in Q2? Please cite the source.
AI assistant
Net profit was $48.3 million, up 12% year on year, according to page 7 of its Q2 earnings release.
Figure and citation fabricated
  • Hallucination: the model generates a plausible figure; it does not retrieve one.
  • Knowledge cutoff: training data end at a fixed date, whereas markets continue to move.
  • Numerical unreliability: numbers are processed as tokens, so NPV and ratio calculations require independent verification.
  • Bias: the model reproduces biases present in its training data.

The institution cited is fictional.

Because the model generates plausible text rather than retrieving facts, it may state false information with apparent confidence (hallucination). Its knowledge ends at a training cutoff date, it may mishandle numerical calculations, and it may reproduce biases in its training data. Figures should always be verified against the primary source.

Applications in financial services

  • Client service: responding to account and product enquiries.
  • Research: summarising earnings calls, regulatory filings and research documents.
  • Compliance: assisting with contract review and identifying high-risk clauses.
  • Productivity: drafting meeting notes, correspondence and preliminary analysis.

Fraud detection and credit scoring usually employ other classes of machine-learning model. Financial professionals remain responsible for verifying and explaining model output.
